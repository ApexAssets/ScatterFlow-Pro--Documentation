Export

Transfer objects and scatter layers from Blender to Unreal Engine or Godot

How it works

ScatterFlow connects Blender to an open Unreal Engine or Godot project, so you can send your scene straight to the engine without exporting and importing files.

Objects such as rocks, buildings, and terrain arrive with their materials. Scatter layers arrive as editable ScatterFlow layers, not thousands of separate objects, so you can keep adjusting them in the engine.

Exporting again updates what you sent before instead of making duplicates.

Install the engine plugin first. The project must be open in the engine with the ScatterFlow plugin installed. This page covers Blender's side; see the Unreal and Godot guides for setting up the engine.

Step 1: Connect to your engine

Pick the engine and click Connect

First open your project in the engine with the ScatterFlow plugin enabled. In Unreal, click ScatterFlow in the main toolbar. In Godot, open the ScatterFlow Pro dock.

Then click the Unreal or Godot button at the top of Blender's Export tab and click Connect. The status light is green when you are connected, orange while connecting, and red when not connected.

Click Disconnect before switching to another engine.

Connection remains red? Check that the engine plugin is running. Then make sure the Link URL field in ScatterFlow's Export preferences matches the address shown in the engine's ScatterFlow panel.

Step 2: Choose what to send

Tick what you want to export

The two checkboxes under Export choose what is sent. Selected Objects sends the objects selected in the viewport. Its count shows which are new and which were sent before and will be updated.

Scatter System sends the layers ticked in the Scatter Layers list. The All and None buttons tick or untick every layer.

Excluding a layer does not delete it from the engine. It just skips that layer's update, so untick layers you have not changed to make the export quicker.

Step 3: Export

Click Export to Unreal

Click Export to Unreal or Export to Godot. Objects and scatter layers can be sent in the same export.

Keep working in Blender and export again whenever you want to update the engine. Anything you sent before is updated in place, not duplicated.

Export settings

Click Settings beside the Export title for optional controls. The defaults suit most scenes.

What is sent along

For objects

The Materials checkbox sends the object's materials. Turn it off if you will build the materials in the engine yourself.

The Collision checkbox adds collision, so characters can stand on surfaces and are blocked by solid objects.

Nanite (Unreal only) turns on Unreal's Nanite, which suits detailed meshes.

For scatter layers

The Emitter checkbox sends the terrain the layers sit on. Turn it off when the terrain is already in the engine and only the layers need updating.

Nanite (Unreal only) turns on Nanite for the terrain and the scattered assets.

The PCG Mode menu sets how a layer starts out in the engine. Exact Mode keeps the instance positions from Blender. Procedural Mode lets the engine regenerate the scatter, so you can adjust density and distribution there. You can switch modes later in the engine.

What is sent, and what is not

Export sends the content listed below. Anything else needs to be set up in the engine.

Sent to the engine

Selected mesh objects, with their materials and optional collision. Materials are baked when needed (see Texture Baking).

Lights, except sun lamps. Collection instances arrive with the collection's meshes.

ScatterFlow scatter layers, with their instances. Very large layers, over 1,000,000 instances or 500,000 for Godot, are streamed in around the camera.

Ivy and water bodies made with ScatterFlow.

Objects set to show only in shadows (Camera off, Shadow on, under Object Properties > Visibility) stay shadow-only in the engine. Objects turned off for rendering arrive hidden.

Not sent: set these up in the engine

The World: sky, HDRI, sky light, exposure, and view transform. Set these up in the engine, or use ScatterFlow Sky in Unreal.

Sun lamps and fog boxes: use the engine's own sun and fog.

Weather: turn on rain and snow with the engine's ScatterFlow controls.

Rivers: water bodies are sent, but river systems are not.

Not sent: other scattering

Only ScatterFlow layers are sent as scatter. Particle systems, other Geometry Nodes setups, and instances from other scatter tools are left out. Convert them to real objects or bring them into ScatterFlow first. GScatter environments can be imported as Biomes.

OpenVDB volumes are also left out, and the export log lists them.

Texture Baking

When your materials need converting

Some Blender materials, such as those with procedural textures, colour ramps, or mixed shaders, cannot be rebuilt in the engine as they are. ScatterFlow turns them into image textures during export. This is called baking.

The settings are in the Texture Baking section, below the Export button. Its header shows which mode is active: Auto, Bake All, or Off.

The Bake on Export if not PBR toggle bakes only the materials that need it. It is on by default and suits most scenes.

The Bake All toggle bakes every material, including image-based ones. Use it when a material looks different in the engine than in Blender.

The Resolution buttons set the size of baked textures. Source matches the material's own images. 2K and 4K are sharper but make larger files.

Raise Samples if baked textures look grainy.

Timeout per Pass (min) limits how long one bake pass may take. If a pass runs over, the object is exported without baking. See When a bake is skipped or fails.

Initial exports can take longer. Baking happens during export. Later exports reuse the baked images unless something has changed.

How baking works

This section explains what gets baked, where the results are kept, and what a baked texture cannot hold.

Which materials are baked

Materials made of image textures plugged into a Principled BSDF are sent without baking. The engine rebuilds them from those textures.

Other materials are baked. This includes procedural textures, Color Ramps, Mix Shaders, shaders other than Principled BSDF, and node groups that change their inputs.

Two kinds of extra nodes do not cause a bake:

Simple image adjustments, such as tiling, offset, tint, brightness, contrast, or roughness changes. The engine applies these itself.

Inactive operations, such as a Mix at factor 0, a Bump at strength 0, muted nodes, or groups with no influence. ScatterFlow looks past them to the textures underneath.

What happens during a bake

Each material channel, such as Base Color, Roughness, and Normal, is baked into its own image. Bump and normal maps are combined into the baked normal map.

Baking runs in the background, so you can keep working in Blender, and a failed bake will not close your session. A progress card in the viewport shows how far it has got. Press Esc to cancel.

Baking uses the GPU set in Blender's Preferences > System, or the CPU if no GPU is set. You can also choose the device with the Device buttons under Texture Baking in ScatterFlow's Export preferences.

Re-exports reuse the bakes

Baked images are saved in a _BakeCache folder in the export's Sync Folder, which is next to your .blend file by default. Later exports reuse them unless the material, the UVs, or the bake settings change.

Objects that share a UV-based material, such as tree variants with the same leaves, also share one bake.

To force a full rebake, delete _BakeCache before exporting.

Ground materials that blend two textures

Terrain materials often blend two tiling textures, such as grass and dirt, with a mask. Baking all of that into one image would lose detail across a large terrain.

Instead, ScatterFlow bakes each texture as a tile and sends the mask separately, and the engine blends them. For this to work, each texture set must use one Mapping node with no rotation, the first UV map, and nothing based on position or normals.

UV maps

Baking uses the object's first UV map, the same one the engine uses.

UVs outside the 0–1 range are wrapped, so repeating textures such as tiled bark or planks bake correctly.

UDIM materials are baked into one image, so their tiles overlap. The export log warns you when this happens.

Complicated materials and node groups

Node groups are fine, but some material effects cannot be stored in a baked image.

Node groups, nested groups and layered shaders

Baking works through node groups and nested groups, so there is no need to ungroup a material before exporting.

Layered materials, such as rock, moss, and dirt blended with masks, are baked as their combined result. Material Outputs inside groups are found too.

Nodes that depend on the camera or the lighting

Fresnel, Layer Weight, Light Path, Camera Data, and Ambient Occlusion can change with the view or lighting. A bake freezes them, so they no longer react to the camera in the engine.

The exception is glass transparency driven by Fresnel or Layer Weight. ScatterFlow rebuilds this in the engine instead of baking it.

Variation per copy or over time

Per-object variation: Object Info (such as Random or Location) and Particle Info can give each copy its own colour in Blender. Copies share one baked image, so this variation is lost. Use separate mesh variants, or the engine's per-instance variation controls.

Animation: animated material values are baked at the current frame, so the animation is lost.

Displacement, volumes and weather

Displacement: material displacement does not change the shape of the exported mesh. Procedural displacement is baked into the normal map as surface detail instead. An image height map on its own does not cause a bake.

Volume shaders: not baked. Set up fog and other volume effects in the engine.

Rain ripples and wetness: ScatterFlow's weather effects are left out of the bake. The engine's weather system adds them live.

When a bake is skipped or fails

A failed bake does not stop the export. The material is sent with whatever textures and values it has.

After exporting, the status bar sums up the result. The Info editor lists any skipped objects and why. For full bake details, open Window > Toggle System Console.

"no UV map"

The object has no UV map to bake onto, so its procedural materials are sent without baking.

Fix: unwrap the object in Edit Mode, for example by choosing UV > Smart UV Project from the menu, then export again.

"invalid (NaN / infinite) coordinates"

The first UV map has broken values, often from an import or a modifier.

Fix: unwrap the object again with UV > Smart UV Project or Unwrap. If a modifier creates the UVs, apply it and check the result in the UV Editor.

"bake timed out"

A bake pass took longer than Timeout per Pass, so the object was sent without baking.

Fix: raise Timeout per Pass. Dense meshes, 4K output, high Samples, and CPU baking all make bakes take longer.

Bakes are very slow

GPU not configured: check Edit > Preferences > System > Cycles Render Devices, and bake on a GPU rather than the CPU where you can.

Bake settings: Samples 4 is enough for most materials. Source resolution keeps your textures at their existing size. A 4K bake takes about four times as long as 2K.

First export: nothing has been baked yet. Later exports only bake what has changed.

Pink or magenta textures

The material is missing an image, often after moving a scene to another computer. ScatterFlow looks for it next to the .blend file and in a textures folder there.

Fix: choose File > External Data > Find Missing Files from the menu, pick the folder with the textures, and export again.

Grainy, blurry or different from Blender

Grainy: raise Samples.

Blurry: raise Resolution. Source uses the largest image in the material, or 1K for fully procedural materials.

Different appearance: turn on Bake All, so the material is baked instead of rebuilt from its textures. If it still looks different, see the limits under Complicated materials and node groups.

An object is not baked at all

Only mesh objects are baked. Curves, text, and other object types are sent without baking.

Fix: convert the object by choosing Object > Convert > Mesh from the menu. Work on a copy if you want to keep the editable original.

Player Preview

Walk through your scene

Player Preview lets you walk through your Blender scene from a player's view before exporting. It does not need the engine or a connection.

Place the viewport where you want to start, then click the Preview button in the Player Preview section. A shortcut card lists the controls:

W A S D moves, the mouse looks around, Shift runs, Space jumps, and Ctrl crouches.
V switches between first-person, third-person, and top-down views.
The mouse wheel sets the camera distance in third-person and top-down views.
Hold the right mouse button to zoom.
H hides the shortcut card. Esc ends the preview and returns to your previous view.

The Settings section below Preview sets the starting camera and position (such as the current view or the 3D Cursor), full screen, movement speed, mouse sensitivity, and collision with scattered objects.

Slow viewport, slow character

Movement speed stays the same whatever your frame rate, down to 20 frames per second. Below that, the character slows down: at 10 frames per second it moves at half speed. Make the viewport faster rather than raising Walk Speed.

What makes a scene slow to draw

Scatter density: lower Viewport Density or use Nano; see Optimization. Renders keep their full density.

Complex meshes: detailed assets repeated many times. Use lighter meshes or proxies while editing.

Modifiers: Subdivision Surface, Geometry Nodes, and other modifiers that recalculate.

Animation: armatures, drivers, simulations, animated objects, and wind.

Shading: heavy materials, lights, or textures in Material Preview or Rendered shading.

Other applications: an open game engine or another 3D app shares the GPU.

Preview settings: turn on Fullscreen Preview, and leave Collide with Scattered Objects off in dense scenes.
